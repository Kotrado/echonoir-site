The Last Ferry to Tamarisk

On election night, an aging ferryman climbs a soot-black newspaper tower to collect his dead brother's notice, and finds a line on it that he never wrote. In a newsroom where every typist's lips move without a sound, the river is still owed a debt.

I brought the *Ellen Orrin* in to the Tamarisk steps at ten minutes to midnight, and I made her fast myself. Two turns round the bollard. A hitch. Then a third turn, for luck. My brother Daniel showed me that knot, though he was the younger by four years, and he never once let me forget it. I had tied it every night for thirty-one years. It seemed a poor night to stop.

It was the night of the county election. The polls had closed at nine, and every other boat on the Orrin had gone in by ten, so the crews could get to the bars and the wireless sets. I was the last ferry. The company kept me on out of habit. I kept myself on for the same reason.

I waited on the wet stones and listened.

The Tamarisk Building is seven floors of soot-black brick on the north bank, and the *Meridian Courier* takes the top three. The presses are in the cellar, below the tide line. They run every night of the year, and you feel them through your boots before you hear them. A slow iron turning. A sound with no hurry in it.

I went in by the side door. The porter's box in the lobby was empty, though his lamp was lit and his evening paper lay open on the stool. A card hung on the lift gate. *Out of Order*, it said, in brown ink gone pale with age. I took the stairs.

On the third landing a boy ran down past me with a bundle of copy under his arm. He did not look at me. On the fourth I stopped to get my breath. On the fifth a girl in a canteen apron was wheeling a tea urn towards the service lift, and she said good evening, and called me sir. I am fifty-eight years old. Daniel was fifty-four when they took him out of the river three weeks ago, and I had still not grown used to being the one who was left.

The newsroom ran the length of the seventh floor. Green-shaded lamps hung low over the desks, and tobacco smoke lay flat under the ceiling. A clock on the end wall said ten past twelve. I took out my father's watch to check it, and it agreed. Nobody looked up as I went down the room, and I was glad of it. I had come for one strip of paper. I meant to be back on my boat inside ten minutes.

The night editor's desk was at the far end, under the clock. A woman sat behind it, narrow and upright, in a grey cardigan buttoned to the throat. A brass plate on the blotter said ALMA PENHALLOW, NIGHT EDITOR. She was marking a galley with a blue pencil, and the pencil did not pause when I stopped in front of her.

"My name's Leonard Kendrick," I said. "I've come for a death notice. My brother, Daniel Kendrick. Your front counter told me on Tuesday the proof would be ready tonight."

"It's ready," said Alma.

She slid a long strip of proof across the blotter without raising her head. It stopped against my knuckles. I looked down and saw his name in black capitals, and his dates, and under them a line I had never written.

"I didn't give you that," I said. "That last line. That isn't mine."

"No, Mr Kendrick." Her pencil went on. "The river set that one."

I waited for her to look up. When at last she did, I found I had been waiting for something smaller than her face. A little ordinary movement. The kind you only notice when it fails to come.

She had not blinked since I walked in.

She did not blink now.

I stayed where I was, at Alma's desk under the clock, with my brother's proof beneath my hand. I should have folded it and gone. Instead I read it again, slowly, because it was Daniel's name and I had paid four shillings for it.

DANIEL KENDRICK, FERRYMAN. Beloved son and brother. Then his dates. Then the line I had not written.

*He crossed on a promise.*

"Sit down if you like," said Alma. She had gone back to her galley. "There's more of it."

"More? I paid for three lines."

"The proof runs long. Turn it over."

I turned it. On the back, in a smaller face, the print went on in close columns, and I held it nearer the desk lamp to read. The lamp burned without the least flicker. I noticed that and put it aside, the way you put aside a stone in your shoe until you have time for it. Then I laid the proof down on her blotter. I wanted both hands free. I could not have told you why.

That was when I noticed the room.

There were eleven of them at the long desks. Seven men in shirtsleeves and braces. Two women with their hair pinned up. A clerk with a pencil behind his ear, and a boy at the end. Every one of them was typing. I had heard it as I came in and taken it for rain on the skylights. It was not rain. It was eleven machines striking together, key for key, and the carriages coming back on the same instant. Eleven bells, rung as one.

Then I looked at their faces, and that was worse.

Their lips were moving. They were not muttering, and they were not chewing. They were shaping words. Each mouth formed a word as each key came down, and not one of them made a sound. A heavy man by the window rounded his lips on something long, and his hands struck it out a moment after.

"It's the draught," I said aloud. I did not know who I was telling. "There's a draught under that door. It gets at the lamps, and the light plays on their faces."

No paper lifted on any desk. The window beside the heavy man was shut and painted over, and had been for years.

"Then it's tiredness," I said. "Men on a long shift fall into a rhythm. Rowers do."

"Yes," said Alma, without looking up. "They do."

Against the far wall was a teletype in a glass case, and as I watched, it began to tick. I went over to it. I wanted something to do with my feet. The paper came up out of the machine in a pale tongue, and I tore off a length and held it to the light.

*River Orrin. 4.10 a.m. Flat calm. A crossing made.*

I looked at my watch. Ten past twelve. It was hours yet to four. And the machine had no wire clerk to tend it. Its cable hung loose from the back of the case and lay coiled on the boards, plugged into nothing.

"It's a custom," I said. My voice had thinned. "Some night-shift joke. For election night."

Nobody answered me. The teletype ticked on. I tore off a second length, and a third, and the third had my family on it.

KENDRICK, MARGIT. My mother, drowned off the coal staithes in the winter of 'twenty-nine. KENDRICK, ILSE, aged seven. KENDRICK, JOSEPH. My father, who stopped speaking the year before he died. KENDRICK, DANIEL. They ran down the paper in a column, one to a line, in the order they had gone.

After each name came the same few words, in the same plain type.

*Voice owed. Not yet paid.*

I counted them. Four lines. Then a fifth, still printing. The letters pressed up out of the machine one at a time while I watched them come.

It was my name.

I went back to Alma's desk with the teletype paper crushed in my fist. The clock above her still said ten past twelve. I looked at my watch again, and it said ten past twelve as well. It had said so, I think, for some while.

"You'll come down with me," I told her. "Now. We'll fetch the porter, and then the police."

Alma laid her blue pencil across the galley. She had stopped marking.

"Sit down, Mr Kendrick," she said. "You've waited long enough for the plain version."

I didn't sit. She told me anyway.

"Your brother was nineteen," said Alma. "It was January, and the Orrin was running ice. Your father was ill, so Daniel took the ferry over alone, with a doctor's bag for a woman in labour on the south bank. Halfway across, the floes closed round the boat. He couldn't make headway. So he spoke to the river. Men do, out there."

"What did he say to it?"

"He said, get me over, and you can have my voice." She folded her hands. "He meant he'd pray. He meant he'd sing hymns at the tiller, or some such. He thought it was a manner of speaking. The river took it as a bargain."

Behind her the typing went on, eleven keys to the beat.

"He crossed," said Alma. "The ice opened in front of him. He lived another thirty-five years. Then three weeks ago he didn't, and the river came looking for what it was owed."

"He's dead," I said. "Whatever he owed, it's settled."

"A man can't pay with a voice he no longer has." She said it kindly, and that was the worst of it. "It doesn't want coin, and it doesn't want blood. It wants its words said. The river dictates, and someone has to take it down, every night until the dawn edition goes to the presses. Then the next night. Then the next."

"For how long?"

"For good."

I looked along the desks. A bald man near the window had a wedding ring worn thin as wire on his typing hand. He had not raised his eyes once since I came in.

"And these?" I said. "Who are they?"

"The ones who were nearest when it asked." Alma pressed the proof flat on her blotter. "It asked your mother, and your sister, and your father, each in turn. None of them would stay to answer. So it kept on asking, and took whoever was close by."

Something touched the toe of my boot. I looked down. A dark thread of river was crossing the boards from the landing door. It ran in a straight line, in no hurry at all. It reached the leg of the nearest desk, divided, and went round it.

The landing door was open. I crossed to it and looked down the stairwell. Six steps below me, the stairs went under. Black flood lay there, flat and still, with the smell of the mud flats rising off it. While I watched, it took the fifth step. Then the fourth.

The presses had stopped. I hadn't noticed when. There was nothing under the floor now at all, and the silence made the keys behind me very loud.

"That's the only way out," I said.

"It is," said Alma. "It always has been."

The flood rose another step, and behind me the teletype began to tick.

I came back to the night editor's desk without meaning to. The flood had reached the third step from the landing. Alma sat as she had sat all night, and the proof lay flat under her hand.

"There must be a way to pay it," I said. "You said yourself it was owed."

"I said it was owed. I never said you could pay it." She turned her head towards the window, slowly, as if her neck had set in one position. "I'm the last it asked, Mr Kendrick. I sit in this chair. I haven't left it, and I haven't tried to, for a very long time. The river wanted a pair of hands to take its words down. It has had mine. And still it isn't satisfied."

"Why not?"

"Because I'm nearly worn through." She lifted her right hand off the proof and showed me. The tips of her fingers were worn smooth, with no prints left on them. "Hands wear out."

Behind us the teletype ticked, and at the end of a line a single bell rang.

I did not answer her. I had begun to look about the room the way a drowning man looks for a rope. There was the door, and the flood behind it. There were eleven desks and eleven mouths moving on one beat. There was the lamp on Alma's desk, a brass thing on a jointed arm, bent low over her typewriter and lighting nothing.

Then a cup rattled.

The girl from the fifth landing had come in at the service door with a tin tray in both hands. She was perhaps seventeen. Her name was stitched on her apron in red thread: KATHLEEN VENABLES. She had brought tea, God help her, eleven cups of it, and the cups were chattering in their saucers because her arms had begun to shake. Her face had gone the colour of proof paper. She told me the stairs were wet. She told me she couldn't hear the presses any more, and asked me what had happened to them.

"Stay where you are," I said. "Don't come any closer."

She came closer. People do.

Alma's eyes moved once to the girl, then back to me. I will not pretend she urged me on. She said nothing at all. It was the silence of a woman who had watched this choice made before, and knew the shape of it, and was only waiting.

I thought of Ilse, who was seven. I thought of my father at the tiller, the winter he stopped speaking. I thought of the flood on the stairs, and how quickly it was climbing.

I crossed the floor in five strides and took Kathleen by the shoulders. The tray went down. China and hot tea went across the boards. I turned her round, walked her backwards to the night editor's desk, and sat her down in the chair.

Alma was simply no longer in it. I never saw her rise.

The chair received the girl. There is no other word for it. Her back met the slats, and her spine straightened. Her hands came up out of her lap as if someone had lifted them by wires, and they came down on the keys. The typewriter woke with one long rattle.

Her lips parted. They made one word, and I knew it, though no sound came. *Please.* Then they began to make the river's words instead.

Above her the brass lamp stirred. It swung down on its jointed arm, slowly, with a small dry creak, until its hot rim lay across the backs of her hands. It pressed them to the keys. It held them there.

Along the room, the eleven mouths changed their beat. They had found a twelfth.

Alma was by the painted window with her back to all of us. At last, she closed her eyes.

Before I went, I bent and drew Daniel's proof out from under the girl's wrist. It slid free without resistance. She did not stir. I folded it once, and again, and put it inside my coat.

• • •

I did not look at Kathleen again. I had learned that much already. You do not look at what you have paid with.

I went out onto the landing with the proof against my ribs and put my hand on the rail. The stairs ran down into the dark, and they were dry. I tried each step with my boot before I trusted my weight to it. At the fourth landing I stopped and listened for the flood, because I had watched it climb. There was none. Below me the presses had started again, and their long iron beat came up through the soles of my boots.

In the lobby I turned and looked back. The bottom stairs were wet, the way stone is wet after a mop has been over it. A dark tidemark ran along the skirting at the height of my knee. That was all. Nothing had climbed those stairs tonight. Nothing had needed to.

"Hello?" I called towards the porter's box. "Is anybody about?"

His lamp burned on. His paper still lay open on the stool, the type black and clean, and I did not let myself read it.

I pushed the street door and went out onto the quay.

It was not yet light. The sky over the Orrin was the colour of old pewter, and the lamps along the embankment were still burning. The *Ellen Orrin* lay where I had left her, bow to the steps, two turns and a hitch on the bollard. I stepped aboard, and she took my weight without a sound.

I cast off the way Daniel had shown me. The loop first, then the long pull in. My hands were steady. I noticed that, and I did not like it.

I started the engine and took her out into the stream.

I have crossed that river some thousands of times, and I know every voice it has. It slaps under the piers. It sucks at the turn by the old cement works. On a windy night it talks along the hull in a low grumble that never quite makes words. That morning it did none of these things. It lay flat from bank to bank. My wake spread behind me in two clean lines, and no sound came off it. I leaned out and put my hand in. The river let me in, and let me go, and kept its mouth shut.

The sun came up as I reached mid-channel. It laid a long band of copper down the river, and I steered along it. The debt is gone, I told myself. I waited to feel it leave me. Nothing in me moved at all.

I made the south bank at a quarter past six and shut off the engine. The quiet came down over the boat.

"It's done," I said aloud, to nobody. "Do you hear me? It's done."

The river took the words without an echo.

I turned and looked back across. From there the Tamarisk Building was small. Its windows had caught the sunrise and gone to gold, every one but a single window on the seventh floor. That one was lit from inside, a steady yellow, and I knew the lamp. Someone sat beneath it. I could make out the shape of her head, bent forward a little. I could see her hands.

They were moving.

And I could hear them. A quarter of a mile of flat river lay between us, and I heard her keys as plainly as if she sat at my elbow. Eleven machines, and a twelfth.

My jaw ached. I put my palm against it and felt it working under the skin. My lips were shaping every word her keys struck. No. They were shaping it first. I knew each word a breath before she set it down.

"Stop," I said.

The word came out. Then my mouth went on without me, quietly, in its own order.

I do not know how long I had been doing it. Since the stairs, perhaps. Since the chair. There had been no sound, and so I had not heard it. I clamped my teeth shut. My mouth went on moving behind them.

Daniel worked the tiller for thirty-five years with his lips going, and I always thought he was praying. Alma was never the voice. She was only the hands. There had always been a mouth as well, out on the river, and it had been empty since the night they took my brother out of the reeds. It was not empty any more.

I took the proof out of my coat, there on the south bank, with the sun full on it. I wanted to see the girl's name in print. A man goes to look at a gravestone for the same reason.

Daniel's notice was at the head of the strip. Kathleen's was set beneath it. Below hers was a third, and the ink came off on my thumb.

It had no name. There was only a space, the width of a short name, left open for the compositor. Under the space, the line was already set.

*Heard it read aloud, and kept still.*

That was what the river had lacked, in all the years Daniel mouthed at it with nobody near enough to hear. It had hands. It had a mouth. It had never had an ear.

Her hands have not stopped since. Nor has my mouth. I have said every word of this in her order, and the voice you are hearing now has not been mine for some time. She is setting down the last of it.

You have kept very still.

I have watched the space on the proof fill while you listened. Letter by letter, in the same plain type.

It's your name.

It fits.
