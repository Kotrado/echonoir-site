The Surveyor Beneath Thorpe Lodge

Hired to save a roofless abbey, Miss Ashworth finds warm candle stubs on bricked-up sills and a plan of a chapel no map admits exists. When a telephone rings in a house with no line, she learns who has been counting those who measure.

I came to Thorpe Lodge on the eighth of November, 1911, in rain, with one trunk and a lantern I had bought in Leeds for the purpose.

The carrier set me down at the gate and would go no further. He did not say why. He took his money and turned the cart in the lane, and I waited with the trunk at my feet and looked at what I had been hired to save.

You will want to know what it looked like, so I shall tell you plainly. Three ranges of grey stone were set round a cloister garth gone to bracken. The roofs were gone. The windows were open to the sky, and the rain went straight through them onto the floors. There had been a fire in 1811, and in the hundred years since, nobody had touched the place.

A woman was waiting under the gatehouse arch. She was stout and past sixty, in a man's oilskin, and she held a ring of keys against her chest.

"Miss Ashworth?" she said.

"Yes. Mrs Orme?"

"Brenda Orme. I keep the gate lodge for the Estate, though I sleep in the village." She did not offer her hand. She offered the keys. "Eleven there. You'll want the brass one for the muniment room. The rest you'll find out."

I took the ring. It was heavier than it looked.

"Has anyone else been in since the fire?" I asked.

"One man." She turned and walked ahead of me into the garth, and I followed with the trunk dragging behind me on its strap. "A surveyor, in 'twenty-one. Eighteen twenty-one, ten years after it burned. He was sent to measure the place for the Estate. Same as you."

"And?"

"He locked himself in the east range." She pointed with her chin. It was the longest of the three, and the best preserved. "Barred the door from inside. He shouted through it for two days. Then he stopped."

"Shouted what?"

"That he'd not open it. That it wasn't to be named." She looked at me at last. Her eyes were pale and quite steady. "They broke the door on the third day. I'm told he'd gone, and the room was empty. But I'm only telling you what's said."

I thanked her. I believe I smiled. I was thirty-four, and I had worked in ruins for eleven years, and I thought I knew what the villages made of them.

She left me at the foot of the night stair with the rain easing. I lit the lantern.

I began that evening with the west range, because it was safest. I paced the dormitory floor and wrote figures in my notebook. Near ten o'clock I came to a chamber off the passage. Its doorway had been bricked up long ago, and a narrow window was cut high in the blocking. I held the lantern to it.

On the sill lay a stub of candle.

I did not touch it at first. I looked, and then I put out one finger. The wax gave under the pressure. It was still faintly warm, and it smelled of tallow.

I did not move for a good while.

Then I told myself that it was mine. I had carried a candle in my coat since Leeds.

"It's mine," I said aloud, to the passage. "I set it down and forgot it. That's all."

I wrote *wax, west range, ten o'clock* in my notebook, and I did not check my coat.

I took the cloister next, and I took it slowly, because I wanted to be tired by morning.

The north walk measured sixty-one feet. The south walk measured sixty feet eleven. That was the fire, or the settling after it.

"Sixty-one," I said, as I wrote it down. "Sixty, eleven." I have always called my figures aloud. It keeps the hand honest.

The lantern hung from a nail I had driven into the crumbling mortar, and every few minutes I moved it along the wall.

On the third night I found the second candle.

It was in a bricked doorway off the chapter house, again on a sill cut high in the blocking. A stub, burned down to a shallow puddle in its own grease. I touched it. Warm. Warm as a cup that has just been put down.

I did not say anything to the passage this time.

On the fourth night there were two more. On the fifth, one in the infirmary passage, and one beneath the dorter stair. Each was a single candle, and each had burned out, and each was soft. It was all quite plain. No smoke on the stone. No footprint in the ash on the floor. No wick left standing. Only the wax, and the warmth in it, and the smell of tallow, which by then I knew before I reached the door.

I began to arrive at those sills before I meant to. My feet had learned the way.

On the fifth night I checked my coat. I turned out every pocket onto the cloister wall: a pencil, a tape, the ring of keys, a handkerchief. There was no candle. There had never been one.

"Well," I said to the empty walk. "There it is."

I put the lining back and buttoned the coat to the neck.

On the sixth day I went looking for paper. Anyone restoring a building ought to know what was drawn before the fire. My letter of commission, signed in Mr Brindle's name, had said the muniment room was sound. It lay behind the library, up eleven steps, with an oak door that stuck in its frame. I put my shoulder to it. It gave all at once, and I was inside, in a room that smelled of mice and old glue.

The presses were full. Most of it was leases and tithe accounts, tied in tape gone brown. But on the lowest shelf, wrapped in oilcloth, I found a roll of plans on linen. I carried them to the window and unrolled them across the sill. I weighted the corners with my notebook, my pencil, and two stones from the floor.

The hand was small and upright. A surveyor's hand. In the corner were a date, 1821, and an initial, and the word *Thorpe*.

I knew the west range at once. I knew the cloister, with its north walk. Then my eye went to the east range, and I went on looking for a long time.

Beneath it, drawn in finer ink, was a stair. It turned twice. At the foot of it lay a chapel, square and low, with an altar on the north wall and a single narrow desk set against the south.

None of the maps sent with my commission showed it.

In the margin, in the same upright hand, were five words. They were pressed harder than the rest, so hard that the nib had split.

*It counts those who measure.*

I read them aloud. I don't know why. My voice came back thin off the plaster.

Then I turned the plan over, as one does, to look for a note on the back. And there, in pencil, fresh and without a smudge, someone had added a tally.

Six strokes. A gap.

And a seventh, not yet finished.

• • •

Brenda Orme came to the cloister at dusk that evening, as she had every evening that week, with a loaf under a cloth and her lantern swinging. I met her by the well-head. I had the plans rolled under my arm, and I had not yet decided to hide them.

She saw them anyway. She looked at the oilcloth, and then at me.

"You've been in the muniment room," she said.

"I've been where the work is."

"Leave the east range alone, Miss Ashworth." She set the bread on the well-head. Her hands were quite steady. "Measure the west. Measure the cloister, the church, the gatehouse. Mr Brindle can have his report, and you can go home to Leeds."

"Why?"

"Because he was my great-grandfather."

I did not say anything. The light was going from the stones.

"The surveyor," Brenda said. "That's his initial in the corner of those plans, if you'd looked. We don't say his name up here. He went in there with his instruments in the autumn of 'twenty-one, and he turned the key from inside. My grandmother was nine. She knelt at that door three days and spoke to it."

"And did it answer?"

"Something did." She pulled her shawl tighter. "My family has kept the keys ever since. Every one of us. Not for the money. The Estate pays us eleven pounds a year, and it wouldn't keep a hen. We keep them so that nobody finishes his drawings."

I should tell you that I laughed. It was a short, ugly sound, and I was ashamed of it at once.

"They're only drawings," I said.

"Then you won't mind leaving them."

I said I would think about it. Brenda looked at me for a long moment. She nodded as one nods to the sick, and went out through the gate without her lantern. I listened to her boots until the lane took them.

Then I did what I had always done when I was afraid. I measured.

I unrolled the plans on the well-head beside her loaf, and set her lantern on one corner to hold them flat. Then I took the steel tape and the plumb line along the outside of the east range. I did not open any door. I paced the walk, and counted the bays, and laid the tape along the foundations. After each figure I went back to the well-head and checked it against his.

They agreed. Sixty-one feet on the north walk, as I had found. Eleven feet four inches from the south corner to the first buttress. The wall at the chapter-house doorway was nineteen inches thick. I had taken that at noon and written it down in pencil. His ink said nineteen.

"A good surveyor measures well," I said aloud. "That's all it proves."

But the east wall, by my tape, ran forty-four feet. On his plan the same wall ran forty-four feet, with a blank stretch in the middle, nine feet wide. My tape had passed over that stretch and found solid brick. On his plan it was a room. I walked it again. The tape said brick. His ink said a door, with a hinge drawn on the left side.

I waited a long while with the tape slack in my hand.

Then I went to the brick and laid my palm flat on it. It was warm.

• • •

At a quarter to midnight I was still at the east wall, alone, with my palm on the brick. It had given something back all that time. Warmth, steady and low, like a loaf drawn from an oven an hour ago.

I should tell you that I did not think of leaving. I thought of the lump hammer in my trunk.

I fetched it, and a cold chisel with it. I read his ink once more by the lantern. "Hinge side," I said. "Then we'll see."

It took me until the church clock in the village struck two. The mortar was older than the fire, and soft with lime. Courses came away in my hands, and I stacked them against the cloister wall, neatly, as I would have done for a client. Behind the ninth course the lantern flame leaned. Air was moving out of the hole, and it smelled of tallow.

I widened the gap until I could pass through it. Then I went in.

A stair went down. Twelve treads, by my count, each worn hollow in the middle. I laid the tape along the first of them out of habit. Nine inches. His plan said nine.

At the bottom lay the chapel. It was low, and the vault had not fallen. Nothing in it was burned. The stalls were whole, and the floor had been swept. I looked for dust, and found none.

"Somebody keeps this," I said.

My voice did not echo. The room took it and kept it.

At the far end was a narrow desk, the sort a draughtsman uses standing, with a tall stool pushed in beneath it. A drawing was pinned to the desk. A pen lay across the paper with the nib still wet. Beside it, a candle burned in a pewter dish.

The wax at its foot had not yet begun to pool.

I went to it. I did not decide to. The drawing showed the east range in section, and it was good work. The cloister bays were drawn true. The stair was there, and the chapel, and a doorway in the chapel's south wall that I had not yet seen. The lettering was mine. I know my own sevens, with the bar through them, and my habit of underlining a dimension twice.

In the box where I put the date, I read the fifteenth of November. I looked at my watch. It was ten minutes past two, on the morning of the fourteenth.

Then I heard the telephone.

Thorpe Lodge had none. The Estate had promised a line, and the only instrument for four miles was at the post office in the village. But above me, in the cloister, something rang. A bell on a brass wire, thin and insistent. I climbed the twelve treads and followed it across the yard to the gate lodge. A lamp was lit in the kitchen window, though no one was there.

A black receiver lay on the kitchen table, off its hook, and a voice came small from it. I picked it up.

"Miss Ashworth? Is that you? It's Gerald Brindle, at the Estate office. I've been trying the inn since nine."

"Mr Brindle," I said. "It's two in the morning."

"Yes, I know. Forgive me. I only learned this evening." He cleared his throat. "Miss Ashworth, I must ask where you got the commission. We never offered it to anyone of that name. We have sent no surveyor to Thorpe Lodge. Not this year, nor in my time."

I held the receiver against my ear. Across the yard I could see the gap I had opened. A light was burning in it.

"Mr Brindle," I said. "Then who has been paying me?"

• • •

I was still in the gate lodge kitchen, with the receiver at my ear. "Then who has been paying me?" I said again, because he had not answered.

The line gave me only a crackle. Then Mr Brindle said, very gently, "Miss Ashworth, I think you should come away from there tonight. I'll send a motor at first light."

"Yes," I said. "Do."

I put the receiver down on the table, and it went on speaking, small and faithful, to the empty room. I did not stay to hear it. I crossed the yard. At the well-head I took the ring of keys from my coat and laid it beside the lantern Brenda had left, where she would find it. I did not need keys where I was going. The light in the gap did not move as I came.

I was not afraid. I had expected to be. I went down the twelve treads and through the chapel, and the candle was burning on the narrow desk exactly as I had left it. The pen lay across the paper. My drawing waited under it, the east range in section, every wall true.

One wall was missing. I saw that at once. A short wall, at the foot of the stair, closing the last gap.

I knew where it went. I knew the length of it as I knew my own name, eleven feet and four inches. I knew I would underline the figure twice, as I always did. You will say I might have walked out. I thought of the motor coming at first light, and Mr Brindle's kind voice, and the commission that no one had given me. Then I thought of the drawing, left as it was.

I drew out the stool and sat. It took my weight as though it had been waiting for it.

"Eleven feet four," I said aloud, and my voice was small in the vault.

I took up the pen. The nib was dry, and then it was not. I set the rule against the paper and drew the line from left to right, slowly, and the wall appeared where it had to be.

Behind me, a long way up, stone moved against stone. It was a soft sound, and patient. A brick, and then another. I did not turn. I underlined the dimension twice.

The candle burned a little straighter. Its flame stopped leaning and rose tall and still, and the wax beneath it ran warm and clear down the side.

At dawn Brenda Orme came across the yard with a fresh loaf in her apron. The gate lodge door was open, and the black receiver lay on its side on the table. She did not go in. She walked on to the east range, to the place where the wall had been broken through the night before, and stopped.

The wall was whole. The brick was sound and the mortar dark with age. The courses had been laid from the far side, for the trowel marks all pointed inward. She put her palm flat against it. It was dry, and quite cold.

On the ledge of the first buttress was a stub of candle, newly burned. When she touched it, the wax was warm.

She went back to the well-head, where the ring of keys lay beside her lantern. She picked it up. She counted the keys once, and then again, with her lips moving. There had always been eleven. She held up the last one, new and bright, with no rust on it and no tarnish. A paper tag was tied through the bow, in a small, upright hand she knew.

It read, *Ashworth*.

Brenda Orme put the ring in her apron. Then she went back to the wall, and set the loaf on the ledge beside the wax.
