The Green Coat Lease

A parcel with no postmark leaves a cataloguer a walnut television that wakes only in the dark, showing a street she knows and a woman who wears her face. Each night the picture runs a little longer, and it begins to ask her for things.

The parcel was lying on the doormat when I came up the stairs at ten past six, squared to the edge of the coir as though someone had measured it. Brown paper, a great deal of string, and my name across the front in a hand I could not place. It was not a clerk's hand, nor Mrs Pettigrew's round capitals from the rent receipts. The letters leaned backwards. The M of Millicent had been made in two strokes, like a pair of tents.

"You've had a visitor," said a voice below me. Marjorie Pettigrew stood on the half-landing with her keys in her fist, looking up. "I heard the door go at four. I looked out, and there was nobody on the stairs."

"The post, perhaps."

"The post doesn't come up in carpet slippers." She said it flatly, and went down again.

There was no stamp and no postmark. I carried the thing inside, set it on the kitchen table, and cut the string with the bread knife before I had taken off my hat.

Under the paper was a sheet of newsprint, folded in four and soft as cloth. Under that was a television. It was small, no bigger than a bread bin, in a walnut case with a cloth grille at the side and two brass knobs gone dull. The glass was grey and slightly convex. I had seen such sets in photographs of the nineteen-fifties, and never one so clean. There was no dust in the grooves of the knobs. A flex lay coiled beside it, tied with a twist of thread, ending in a plug with two round pins.

I turned it over. There was no maker's plate, no number, no label of any kind. I read what I could of the newsprint by the kitchen lamp, a column of cattle prices, an advertisement for a corset, and then I stopped, because I was very tired. I folded it again. I put the television on the sideboard in the front room, with the flex hanging loose, and I did not plug it in. I would like that to be understood.

I ate bread and cheese standing at the window. At half past nine I went round the flat putting out the lamps, until only the one on my bedside table remained. I wound my watch. I put out the lamp.

The dark in that flat is a complete one. The shutters fit well, and the window gives on to the blind wall of the chandler's yard.

For some seconds I heard only the pipes. Then a faint hiss came through the open door, steady as a kettle a long way off, and a dull light began to move across the ceiling.

I went through in my nightgown. The flex still hung over the edge of the sideboard, its plug swinging a little above the floor. The glass was lit.

It showed a street in a grey without any colour at all, wet, with a gutter down the middle. Terraced houses leaned in on both sides, and I knew the shape of the chimney pots. A woman in a long green coat, the only colour on the screen, was walking away from me towards a door at the end.

I knew the door. I could not think from where. She put her hand to the knocker, and stopped, and began, very slowly, to turn her head.

She did not turn far. I saw the line of her cheek, grey as the street, and then the picture dissolved into white lines, and the glass went black. I stood there until my feet were cold. Then I lay on the bed with my eyes open, and did not once put out my hand to the lamp.

In the morning I examined the thing as I would any item brought to me for listing. The veneer was lifting at one corner. The back panel was held by four slotted screws, and I took them out with a kitchen knife. Inside were valves, a cracked condenser, and a good deal of dust. No cable ran to anything. The flex ended in a plug, but its other end ended in nothing at all, merely cut and bound with black tape gone brittle. I put the screws in a saucer and drew back the shutters. The set sat on the sideboard, a dead brown box, and reflected the window.

That night I tried it again, and the night after, with variations. I covered the keyhole. I stopped the gap under the door with a towel. I left a candle burning in the passage, and the glass stayed blank until I pinched the wick. Then it came on, and the street was the same street, and the woman was further on. On the second night she knocked. On the third, the door opened inwards on a dark hall, and she stood a long while looking into it. Each night gave me perhaps four minutes more than the last. I timed it by my watch, holding the watch an inch from my face.

On the fourth morning I knew the street. I was buying a loaf at Dunmore's, and I saw, across the road, the leaning chimney pots. Candlewick Lane. I walked down it with the loaf under my arm and counted my paces. It took four minutes, at an ordinary gait, from my own door to the door at the end. It was shut. It had a brass knocker in the shape of a hand. I did not touch it.

Marjorie Pettigrew has kept the keys to St Ninian's for thirty years, and has the second-floor front, and gives tea on Thursdays whether she is asked or not. I went down that afternoon. Her room smelled of coal and lavender water. She poured with a steady hand and told me about the damp in the cellar.

"Who had my flat before me?" I said.

"Oh, a woman. Quiet." She stirred her cup. "Kept very odd hours. Burned no lights at all, you know. I used to look up from the yard and the windows were always black. I said to her once, you'll ruin your eyes, and she said she saw better."

"Might I look at the tenancy book? Only for the dates. It is my trade."

Marjorie put down her spoon. The clock on her mantel was loud in the pause. She looked, not at me, but at the cupboard door on the landing, which stood open a hand's breadth behind me; I knew this because I saw her eyes go past my shoulder.

"The pages for that flat were taken out," she said. "A long time ago. I couldn't tell you by whom."

"But you keep the book."

"I keep what's left of it," said Marjorie Pettigrew, and poured me more tea, though my cup was full.

I did not sleep that night, or not in the way I understood sleep. The set came on at ten to ten, as it had on every night since the first, the moment I put out the lamp. I lay with my face turned to it.

The woman in the green coat came through the street door of St Ninian's. I knew the door by its cracked fanlight, and the boot-scraper bent at one end. She did not look about her. She climbed. The camera did not follow her. It hung fixed in the corner above the third-floor landing, and it received her from above, so that I saw the parting of her hair and the top of her hat and no face. She stopped at my door. It opened for her without a key.

Then the picture changed to the inside of my flat, from the same high corner, which I measured against my own ceiling and found exact. The fireplace. The bookcase with its two shelves of parish registers. The window, and the yard beyond it. In the middle of the floor, where I have only ever kept a card-table folded against the wall, stood a round table of dark oak on a single carved pedestal. She laid upon it a white cup and saucer. She laid beside them a lamp with a green glass shade, and did not light it. She sat down in front of both and put her hands flat on the wood.

I am a cataloguer, and I did what I am trained to do. I wrote down what I had seen, in pencil, on the back of a Diocesan circular, with the time. Oak. Pedestal. Four claw feet. Green shade. Then I watched her sit until the screen went out at ten to one.

At nine the next morning Marjorie knocked, carrying a tray, since I had missed her Thursday habit and she meant to correct it. She found me at the desk with the telephone beside me and a furnisher's catalogue from Dunbar Road open at my elbow. I do not remember sending for the catalogue. I remember the page, and my finger on it, and the voice of a young man reading back my order: oak, round, pedestal, four feet, delivery Friday. "And the lamp, madam?" I had said that I had a lamp.

"Oak," said Marjorie, reading over my shoulder. Her tray did not tremble. "Round."

"You needn't say it like that."

"I'm saying it how it is." She set the tray down and looked at the card-table against the wall. "I'd want it measured, if I were you. Before it comes."

I rang the newspaper archive at Queen's Wharf that afternoon. The wrapper I had kept flat between two boards; I read them the masthead and the date, and a notice, third column, which I had not troubled to read until then. I was put through to a Miss Drummond, who said she was the oldest clerk they had and the only one who still knew the shelving. She was gone eleven minutes. When she came back she was breathing hard.

"I have the volume open, Miss Holloway. The notice is there. 'Miss M. Holloway, cataloguer, St Ninian's. Interred on the fourth.' That is the wording."

"Then it is a coincidence of names."

"The address is not a coincidence, my dear." Paper moved on her end. "This number is dated the fifteenth. You say the burial was on the fourth. Eleven days. They printed her funeral after she was in the ground, and the notice was placed, it says here, by instruction of the deceased."

I did not mean to sleep that night. On the ninth evening I carried the lamp from the writing desk to the new oak table, which had come on the Friday as promised, and set it down beside the cup I had put out without thinking. Then I sat. I had made up my mind, if that is the phrase, on the strength of nothing but temper. If the set lit only in darkness, I would give it none.

The lamp was a brass one with a green shade and a forty-watt bulb. I had owned it eleven years. By ten o'clock I had read the same page of a Kirkcudbright burial register four times. By midnight I had stopped reading and was watching the walnut case, its blind grey face turned to the room. The radiator knocked twice and went quiet. Somewhere below, a door closed in the stairwell, and I counted the steps until I could no longer hear them.

At twenty minutes to two the screen woke.

There was no click. The glass went from grey to white and then to the street, Candlewick Lane, wet, with a gas lamp at the corner, though there has been no gas lamp there since the war. My own lamp burned on beside it. I put my hand flat on the table and felt the grain of the oak, and was grateful for it.

The picture moved indoors, to the stairs, then to my door, then to the room in which I sat. The fixed eye near the ceiling looked down upon a table, a cup, a lamp with a green shade. A woman sat at it in a green coat, her back half to me. She had been sitting, I understood, for some while.

She turned her head. She looked into the lens, and so at me, and her face was mine, only rested, and without any fear in it at all. She lifted a slip of paper in two fingers and held it to the glass. I rose and went close. It was a page torn from a ledger, ruled in blue. Across the top was written tomorrow's date. Below it, a line of tenancy in a hand I knew from thirty Christmas cards: Marjorie Pettigrew, for the landlord. And then, where the tenant's name should be, mine, not yet signed, the pen waiting on the line.

I was on the landing before I had put on my shoes.

Marjorie opened at the third knock, in a quilted dressing gown, with her keys already in her hand, as if she had been standing behind the door. She looked at my face, and was not at all surprised, and stepped back to let me in. I asked her nothing. I said the date, and the word lease.

"Three times," said Marjorie Pettigrew. Her voice was perfectly level. "I have signed your name three times, Millicent. Each time for a woman who went out in a green coat one evening and was not seen again."

"You knew. You poured my tea and you knew."

"I knew what I was paid to know. I've had thirty years to learn what I wasn't." She turned the keys over once in her palm. "And I liked you. That's the part I can't mend."

"And the television?"

"It always comes back to the cupboard on the landing," she said. "I have never once had to put it there."

I carried the set down myself. Marjorie did not offer to help, and I did not ask her. It was heavier than I remembered, or I was lighter; I could not tell which. I had wound up the flex in the afternoon, though it had never needed a plug, and wrapped the whole in the brittle newsprint, folding the corners as I had found them. The paper left a grey dust on my cuffs.

The cupboard on the landing was narrower than a wardrobe and smelled of camphor and cold plaster. Marjorie unlocked it with the third key on her ring, without looking for it. Inside were two shelves of parish ledgers, a tin of floor wax, and, on the upper shelf, side by side, two parcels in grey paper.

They were the same size as mine. The newsprint on each had gone the colour of weak tea, and the string was knotted in the manner I use myself, a double turn and a slip. I did not touch them. I set my parcel on the shelf beside them, squared it to the edge, and found that I had left a space of exactly two inches between each, as one does when arranging books.

"Shut it," said Marjorie from her doorway. "Don't stand about with it open."

"Marjorie. If I go now, tonight, and take a train—"

"Then you'll be the one who went. They all went." She did not raise her voice. "Shut it, Millicent."

I shut it. The latch was stiff and made a small dry sound like a knuckle. She locked it, put the keys into her pocket, and went in without another word. I heard her draw the bolt.

That morning, before any of this, I had walked to Candlewick Lane and bought a coat. I say bought. I remember the shop, a narrow outfitter's with a brass bell, and I remember counting out four pound notes and some silver. I do not remember choosing the colour. It was green, a dull bottle green, and it fitted across the shoulders as if cut from my measure. The assistant said it had been in the window some while.

At six I put it on. I had not meant to. I stood in the kitchen with the lamp lit, intending to wait for morning and the agent's office and a train, and found my arms already in the sleeves and the buttons done up to the throat.

I went out onto the landing and down to the half-landing, where the stairwell turns and the rail is worn pale by thirty years of hands. I stopped there. I put my right hand on the rail. The timer light on the floor below clicked off, as it does after two minutes, and the whole well went dark.

Then I heard it. Not music, nor voices; only the faint high note a set makes when it warms, once, twice, a third time, each slightly apart from the others.

A seam of light appeared under the cupboard door, and widened, and was grey, and was steady. I could see my shoes in it. I did not need to go back and look. I knew what the three small screens were showing, because I could feel, between my shoulder blades, the exact place where each lens was resting.

I took my hand from the rail, and began, one step at a time, to go down.
